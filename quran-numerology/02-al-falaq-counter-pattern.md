# Surah Al-Falaq as a counter-pattern to sorcery and spiritual attack

Part 2 of the Mu'awwidhatayn study. [Part 1](01-surah-al-falaq.md) covered the numbers, letters and structure. This
part asks a sharper question:

> Is Al-Falaq built as a **counter-pattern**, a deliberate inversion of how evil, sorcery and spiritual attack work?
> If so, what exactly does it invert, and where do the numbers fit in?

The same tags are used: **[Fact]** (textual or computed), **[Tradition]** (hadith or classical scholarship),
**[Reflection]** (interpretation for contemplation).

---

## 0. The short answer

**What the evidence supports:** Al-Falaq's words, structure and prescribed use line up point by point *against*
the anatomy of sorcery, as that anatomy is described in four independent bodies of knowledge:

1. The Quran and hadith.
2. Classical Muslim scholars who studied sorcery, notably Ibn al-Qayyim and Ibn Khaldun, who saw it practised.
3. The history of "binding magic" in the ancient Near East and Mediterranean.
4. Modern behavioural science on curses and fear.

The match is close enough to call the surah a counter-pattern (§3).

**What the evidence does not support:** the idea that the *numbers themselves* are the mechanism. In every Islamic
source, protection comes **from Allah**, reached through the act of seeking refuge in Him. The sources also say
something sharper. Turning numbers or letters into a protective *code* or object is the very method that sorcery and
talismans use. Doing so would rebuild the pattern the surah exists to break (§6).

So the numbers are best read as the **signature of the design**, the visible geometry of a protection whose power
lies elsewhere. That reading is still rich, and it is the one that holds up.

## 1. The knowledge base: how spiritual attack is described

### 1.1 The Quran and Sunnah model **[Fact / Tradition]**

| Property of the attack | Text |
|---|---|
| **It is created.** Every harmful agent is a creature. | *min sharri **ma khalaq*** (113:2) |
| **It needs permission.** Sorcerers "cannot harm anyone except by Allah's leave". | 2:102 |
| **It has limited jurisdiction.** Satan "has no authority over those who believe and rely on their Lord. His authority is only over those who take him as patron and who associate others with Allah." | 16:99–100 (immediately after the command to seek refuge, 16:98); also 15:42 |
| **It enters through heedlessness.** "Whoever turns away from the remembrance of the Most Merciful, We assign him a devil as companion." | 43:36 |
| **It depends on time.** "When night falls, keep your children in, for the devils spread out at that time." | Bukhari 3280, Muslim 2012 |
| **It uses specific channels:** knots and breath (113:4), the envious eye (113:5; "the evil eye is real", Bukhari 5740), and the whisper (114:4–5). | — |
| **It works through illusion and terror.** "They bewitched the people's eyes and **terrified** them" (7:116); "it seemed to him, from their sorcery, that the ropes were moving" (20:66). | 7:116, 20:66 |
| **It is ultimately void.** "Allah will nullify it" (10:81); "the sorcerer will not succeed wherever he comes" (20:69). | 10:81, 20:69, 7:118 |
| **It has a cosmic boundary.** The heaven is guarded, and devils who try to eavesdrop are driven off by flames. Stolen fragments reach soothsayers, who mix in a hundred lies. | 15:16–18, 37:6–10, 67:5, 72:8–9; Bukhari 3210 |

### 1.2 Classical Muslim scholarship on the soul and sorcery **[Tradition]**

- **Ibn al-Qayyim** (*Zad al-Ma'ad*, the chapter on Prophetic medicine) explains the evil eye as harm sent from the
  envier's soul. He compares it to an arrow. If it meets a person who is **uncovered, with no protection**, it takes
  effect. If it meets a person who is **guarded and armed**, with no opening for arrows, it fails, and it may even
  return to the one who shot it. He also explains the Prophetic **nafth** (light blowing during ruqya). The reciter's
  soul and breath meet the evil souls, much as those souls work through their own blowing and "sting".
- **Ibn Khaldun** (*Muqaddimah*, ch. VI, "The sciences of sorcery and talismans") describes sorcery he **saw
  himself**. The practitioner makes a figure standing for the victim and speaks foul words over it. He gathers spittle
  and **blows** it while repeating the sounds of those words. Then he **ties a knot**, "as an omen of binding and
  fastening", and takes an oath from the jinn who share in the act. A malignant spirit, he says, leaves with the breath
  attached to the spittle. This matches *an-naffathat fi'l-'uqad* almost word for word. In the same chapter he
  discusses letter and number magic (*simiya'*) as a related occult craft.
- **Al-Qurtubi**, on 113:5: envy (*hasad*) was the **first sin** committed in heaven (Iblis envying Adam) and the first
  on earth (Qabil envying Habil).

### 1.3 Comparative history of binding magic **[Fact: documented scholarship]**

- **Greco-Roman curse tablets** (*katadesmos* in Greek, *defixio* in Latin). The names come from the words for
  **"bind"** and **"pierce/fix"**. More than 1,500 survive, from Britain to North Africa. They were sealed with nails
  and **hidden in graves, wells and springs**; late Athenian examples cluster in **wells**. John Gager's main
  categories of motive are competitions, lawsuits, love and business. These are mostly forms of **rivalry**, in
  other words envy.
- **Mesopotamian *Maqlû*** ("Burning"). This is the major anti-witchcraft ritual of the ancient Near East. It ran
  through **a single night and into the following morning**, burned figurines of the witch, sent the witchcraft
  **back** onto its maker, and ended as a kind of trial that acquitted the victim.
- **Pre-Islamic Arabia.** Two practices are recorded:
  - Blowing on knots (113:4).
  - When a traveller camped at night in a deserted valley, he would say **"أعوذ بسيد هذا الوادي من شر سفهاء قومه"**:
    "I seek refuge with the master of this valley (a jinn chief) from the evil of the foolish ones among his people".
    The tafsir of 72:6 (al-Tabari, al-Baghawi, al-Qurtubi, Ibn Kathir) records this. The Quran's verdict is that
    *"they only increased them in **rahaq**"*. Early commentators glossed *rahaq* as burden or sin (Ibn Abbas, Qatada)
    and as **fear** (al-Rabi' ibn Anas).

### 1.4 Behavioural science: the human layer **[Fact: published research]**

These findings cannot measure jinn or sorcery. They do describe how belief in an attack acts on a person.

- **Fear magnifies harm.** Walter Cannon's classic paper *"'Voodoo' Death"* (1942) documented how belief that one has
  been cursed can produce terror strong enough to cause physical collapse. This is the extreme end of the **nocebo**
  effect. Note that the Quran names *terror* (7:116) as a tool of sorcery.
- **Naming a threat calms the alarm system.** Putting a feeling into words ("affect labeling") lowers amygdala
  activity (Lieberman et al., 2007).
- **Ritual lowers anxiety.** Performing a ritual reduced anxiety and improved performance under stress
  (Brooks et al., 2016).

## 2. The anatomy of an attack

Putting the four sources together, sorcery and spiritual attack share a common anatomy:

| # | Component | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| A | **Power source:** a pact with, or appeal to, spirits | 72:6; Ibn Khaldun's "oath with the jinn"; curse tablets addressed to spirits of the dead and underworld gods |
| B | **Concealment:** hidden words and buried objects | the Prophet's ﷺ sorcery was hidden in the **well** of Dharwan (Bukhari 5763); curse tablets were hidden in **wells** and graves |
| C | **Binding:** knots, nails, "I bind…" | 113:4; Ibn Khaldun's knot "as an omen of binding"; *katadesmos* = "binding" |
| D | **Darkness:** night, underground places | 113:3; Bukhari 3280; Maqlû's night-time setting |
| E | **Breath and word as the carrier** | *naffathat*; Ibn Khaldun's spittle-laden breath |
| F | **Targeting:** an image of the victim, their name, their personal items | the comb and hair in the Prophet's ﷺ case (Bukhari 5763); Ibn Khaldun's figure of the victim |
| G | **Motive:** envy and rivalry | 113:5; Gager's catalogue of curse motives |
| H | **Exploiting gaps:** unguarded hours, a heedless person, a frightened mind | 43:36; Ibn al-Qayyim's "uncovered" target; 7:116 "terrified them"; nocebo research |

## 3. Al-Falaq's counter to each component

| # | Attack | Al-Falaq's counter | Tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Appeal to spirits | **The same verb, redirected:** *a'udhu bi-**Rabb*** | Fact + Tradition |
| B | Concealment | It opens with **قُلْ, "Say!"**: spoken aloud, openly | Fact |
| C | Binding | **Falaq, "splitting open"**; every verse ends in a burst-release sound | Fact + Reflection |
| D | Darkness | **The Lord of the Daybreak**; recited at dawn and dusk | Fact + Tradition |
| E | Breath as carrier | The **Prophetic nafth**: the same act, filled with revelation | Tradition |
| F | Targeting | **Names no one** and needs no knowledge of the attacker | Fact + Reflection |
| G | Envy | **Names envy, exposes it, and places it last**, as the root | Fact + Tradition |
| H | Gaps | **A general clause plus a continuous practice timetable** | Fact + Tradition |

### A. The power source: same verb, opposite address. This is the strongest finding.
**[Fact]** The root **ع-و-ذ** (seeking refuge) occurs **17 times** in the Quran. **Sixteen** of them seek refuge in
Allah. **Exactly one** seeks refuge in something else, **72:6**, *"men of mankind sought refuge in men of the jinn"*.
That one is also the only instance with a stated bad outcome: *"so they increased them in rahaq"* (burden, sin, fear).

Now set the pre-Islamic night formula beside Al-Falaq:

| | Verb | in whom | from | the evil of |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Jahiliyya (72:6 tafsir) | أعوذ | **بسيد هذا الوادي** (the master of this valley) | من | شر سفهاء قومه (the evil of the foolish ones among his people) |
| Al-Falaq 113:1–2 | أعوذ | **برب الفلق** (the Lord of the Daybreak) | من | شر ما خلق (the evil of what He created) |

**[Reflection]** The grammar is the same: *I seek refuge – in the master of X – from the evil of Y*. Al-Falaq changes
one slot. It replaces a local jinn "master of a valley" with **the Lord who created the jinn and the valley**. It also
widens the threat from "his foolish people" to **"everything He created"**, which takes in the jinn chief himself. The
night-traveller's formula was a **contract inside creation**. Al-Falaq takes the refuge **outside and above creation**.
That is why it cannot be outbid: by 2:102 and 16:99–100, every power the attacker draws on works only by permission
of the One the reciter has already gone to.

**[Fact]** Among the people whose words the Quran quotes, **Musa** uses the root most, three times (2:67, 40:27,
44:20). He is the prophet who faced Pharaoh's sorcerers. Twice he says **عذت بربي**, "I sought refuge in my Lord",
with the same *bi-Rabb* construction as Al-Falaq. (The Prophet Muhammad ﷺ is *commanded* to seek refuge 8 times.)

### B. Concealment answered by "Say"
Sorcery hides: in wells (Dharwan; the Athenian wells), in graves, in mumbled words. **[Fact]** The first word of the
surah is the command **قُلْ**, "Say it". The Mu'awwidhat are recited aloud and wiped over the body.
**[Reflection]** The attack depends on staying hidden. The counter is a public, spoken declaration of whose protection
you are under.

### C. Binding answered by splitting
- **[Fact]** Every Quranic use of **ف-ل-ق** is Allah *breaking something open to release life, light or rescue*: the
  seed (6:95), the dawn (6:96), the sea for Musa (26:63). A knot closes; a *falq* opens.
- **[Fact]** Each of the 5 verses ends on a **qalqala** letter (ق ق ب د د). These are stops, where the airflow is
  fully **closed and then released** with an audible burst. **[Reflection]** Each verse acts out, in sound, a closure
  being burst open: a knot coming undone, five times.
- **[Fact]** In Arabic lexicography (Ibn Faris, *Maqayis al-Lugha*), the root **ف-ل-ح** (*falah*, success) has
  "splitting" as one of its original senses; a farmer, *fallah*, is one who splits the soil. The Quran gives a short
  exchange about the sorcerers. They boast **"قد أفلح اليوم من استعلى"** ("whoever prevails today has succeeded",
  20:64). The answer comes twice: **"ولا يفلح الساحر حيث أتى"** (20:69) and **"ولا يفلح الساحرون"** (10:77).
  **[Reflection]** The sorcerer is denied the *breakthrough*. The believer seeks refuge in the *Lord of the
  breakthrough*.

### D. Darkness answered by daybreak
- **[Fact]** Sorcery's time is the night: devils spread at nightfall (Bukhari 3280), and the jinn-refuge formula was
  used at night (72:6). Even the Mesopotamian counter-ritual is a night that ends in morning.
- **[Fact]** In Arabic, **سِحْر** (sorcery) shares its letters with **سَحَر**, the last dark hour before dawn (3:17,
  51:18, 54:34). Ibn Faris lists these as separate origins of the root, so this is not an etymology.
  **[Reflection]** The pairing is still apt. *Sahar* is the twilight where dark and light blur; *falaq* is the moment
  that blur is **split** into clear day. The Quran fills the *sahar* hour with the righteous seeking forgiveness
  (51:18), and Lut's family was rescued "**bi-sahar**", at pre-dawn (54:34).
- **[Tradition]** The Sunnah places the surah at **both** boundaries of the night: morning and evening, three times
  each (Abu Dawud 5082), and again at bedtime (Bukhari 5017).

### E. Breath answered by breath
**[Fact]** النفاثات (113:4) is the **only** occurrence of the root ن-ف-ث in the Quran. The hadith describing how the
Prophet ﷺ used this surah, however, uses **the very same verb**. Every night he joined his palms, **نَفَثَ فيهما**
("blew lightly into them"), recited Al-Ikhlas, Al-Falaq and An-Nas, and wiped his body, three times (Bukhari 5017). He
did the same over himself in his last illness (Bukhari 5016).
**[Reflection]** The sorcerer's channel is breath carrying a foul word into a knot. The Prophetic counter **uses the
same channel and reverses its content**: breath carrying revelation onto one's own body. Ibn al-Qayyim explains the
practice in these terms. A few early figures disliked *nafth* in ruqya (it is reported from 'Ikrima). The majority
followed the Prophet's ﷺ practice.

### F. Targeting answered by needing no target
- **[Fact]** Sorcery is aimed. Ibn Khaldun's sorcerer builds a figure of the victim. The Prophet's ﷺ case used his
  **comb and hair** (Bukhari 5763).
- **[Fact]** Al-Falaq names **no person, no jinn and no secret name**. Its scope is "**what He created**", which covers
  attackers the reciter does not know exist.
- **[Tradition]** In the Prophet's ﷺ own case, the cure was **not credited to undoing the object**. 'A'isha asked
  whether he would take the bundle out or expose it. He said: *"Allah has cured me, and I disliked to stir up evil
  among people."* The well was then filled in (Bukhari 5763). Other wordings differ on whether the bundle was
  extracted, but all of them credit the cure to Allah.
- **[Reflection]** Magic must know its target. The refuge does not need to know its attacker.

### G. Envy: named, exposed, and placed last
- **[Fact]** The four evils narrow, in order, from the widest circle to the innermost source:

  | Verse | Evil | Grammatical scope |
  |---|---|---|
  | 2 | مَا خَلَقَ | **everything** (universal relative) |
  | 3 | غَاسِق | a **phenomenon** (darkness) |
  | 4 | ٱلنَّفَّٰثَٰت | a **group** of agents |
  | 5 | حَاسِد … حَسَدَ | **one** person, at **one** moment, in **one** heart |

- **[Reflection]** The surah traces the attack **back to its origin**. Sorcery is the *method*; envy, a disease of the
  heart, is the *motive* that starts it. Rival-driven curse tablets and Labid's sorcery both begin there. Al-Qurtubi
  notes that envy was the first sin in heaven and on earth. The surah's **last word** is **حَسَدَ**.
- **[Fact]** حسد (72) and حاسد (73) are the two **lowest-valued content words** in the surah's abjad.
  **[Reflection]** The smallest-looking evil is the root of the chain.
- **[Tradition]** Jibril's ruqya over the Prophet ﷺ follows the same pattern: *"…from the evil of every soul or **envious
  eye**, may Allah cure you"* (Muslim 2186).

### H. Gaps answered by complete coverage
- **[Fact]** The structure is **one general clause plus three specific ones**. The specific clauses cover the three
  hidden vectors: time (darkness), technique (sorcery) and heart (envy). The general clause covers **everything
  else**, including what nobody has named.
- **[Fact]** **أعوذ** is the only *imperfect* verb in the surah, the form for ongoing action. The verbs attached to
  evil (خلق، وقب، حسد) are perfect forms. After إذا they mark a single conditional event.
  **[Reflection]** Refuge is a **continuous state**; the attack is an **event**.
- **[Tradition]** The prescribed timetable leaves no unguarded interval: after each of the 5 prayers (Abu Dawud 1523),
  morning ×3, evening ×3, and bedtime ×3 with nafth.
- **[Tradition]** This is the "fortress" the hadith describes: *"the servant does not protect himself from Satan except
  by the remembrance of Allah"*, like a man who reaches a fortified stronghold ahead of pursuers (Tirmidhi 2863).
  Ibn al-Qayyim's "uncovered target" is exactly the gap that 43:36 warns against.
- **[Reflection, with the science]** The surah also defuses the **terror** channel. It **names** each hidden threat
  precisely, which is affect labeling. It turns the person from victim into actor ("**Say**"). It is a short, fixed
  ritual, which lowers anxiety. And it reassigns control to the One above the attacker. The wrong refuge (72:6)
  "increased them in *rahaq*", fear. The right one does the opposite.

### The asymmetry that defines the counter-pattern
**[Fact / Reflection]** Most counter-magic is **symmetrical**: it fights on the same level as the attack. Maqlû burns
the witch's figurines and sends the witchcraft back onto her. Al-Falaq **contains no curse at all**. It does not name
the sorcerer, return the harm, or bind the binder. The Prophet ﷺ declined even to stir up the matter
(Bukhari 5763). The surah does not out-fight the pattern of magic. It **leaves that pattern's jurisdiction
entirely**. This is why it is a *counter*-pattern rather than a stronger spell.

## 4. The cosmic ("extra-terrestrial") layer

- **[Fact]** The Quran describes the heavens as **guarded** (*hifzan*). It says jinn once rose to eavesdrop and now
  find "**strong guards and flames**" (72:8–9; 37:6–10; 67:5). The one celestial body tied to Al-Falaq is the
  **moon**. The Prophet ﷺ pointed to it and said: *"Seek refuge in Allah from the evil of this; this is al-ghasiq when
  it sets"* (Tirmidhi 3366).
- **[Reflection]** In the Quranic picture the cosmos itself is a **protected structure** with a barrier against
  devils. The Mu'awwidhatayn give a human being the same status on a personal scale. Their scope, *ma khalaq*, does not
  stop at the earth.
- **Candid note:** neither the Quran nor the hadith speaks of "aliens" in the modern sense. The unseen beings it
  describes are the **jinn** and **shayatin**. To extend the surah's protection to anything else harmful in creation,
  rely on the general clause, *"the evil of what He created"*, not on a specific text.

## 5. Where the numbers fit

In this light, Part 1's numerical findings are best read as **structural signatures of the counter-pattern**:

| Signature | What it encodes |
|---|---|
| **1 refuge : 4 evils** | One jurisdiction over all evils, so one refuge is enough |
| **16 : 1** (refuge in Allah vs in jinn, root ع-و-ذ) | The Quran's own tally of the right and the wrong refuge, and the outcome of each |
| **أعوذ = 777**, occurring **7×** in the Quran | The Sunnah's protective seven: 7 'ajwa dates against sihr (Bukhari 5445); *a'udhu* said 7× over pain (Muslim 2202) |
| **3 specific evils**, recited **3×** | Three hidden vectors (time, technique, heart), and three recitations at each boundary of the day |
| **5 of 5 verse endings are qalqala** | Closure → burst: knots undone, in sound |
| **Narrowing order: universal → one heart** | The attack traced back to its root (envy) |
| **11 verses** (Al-Falaq + An-Nas) | The 11 knots of the Labid narration. The chain is weak (al-Baghawi, from Muqatil and al-Kalbi), so use it as a symbol |
| **Odd counts in practice** (1, 3, 7) | "Allah is *witr* (odd) and loves *witr*" (Bukhari 6410, Muslim 2677) |

**These are signatures of design, not switches.** None of the sources says the abjad sums produce the protection.

## 6. Where the counter-pattern breaks: numbers turned into a code

This is the most important point for anyone building on numerology.

**[Tradition]**
- *"Ruqa (pre-Islamic incantations), amulets (**tama'im**) and love-charms are shirk"* (Abu Dawud 3883).
- *"There is no harm in ruqya as long as it contains no shirk"* (Muslim 2200).
- *"Whoever hangs something on himself is entrusted to it"* (Tirmidhi 2072, graded hasan).
- Ibn Khaldun classes letter and number magic, the craft of abjad squares (*awfaq*) and talismans, among the occult
  practices, next to sorcery.

**[Reflection]** Set the two patterns side by side:

| | Magic pattern | Al-Falaq pattern |
|---|---|---|
| Form | hidden code, object, written square | spoken, understood words |
| Address | spirits or the "power" of letters and numbers | Allah alone ("Rabb") |
| Requirement | the correct secret formula | sincerity and trust (*tawakkul*) |
| Timing | a one-off operation | a continuous state |
| Reliance | "entrusted to the object" | entrusted to Allah |

If the numbers of Al-Falaq are made into a *formula*, for example a 777 amulet or a "1111 activation", every row
flips to the left column. The counter-pattern turns back into the pattern. Its value as protection lies precisely in
**not** being a code.

**How to use the numbers well:** use them for *tadabbur*, as focal points that make the meaning stick. "777": the
word of the prophets under siege. "Qalqala": the knot snapping. "16:1": choose the right refuge. Use them to notice
and remember, never as a device.

| As a habit | "Seek refuge with these two; no one has sought refuge with anything like them" | Abu Dawud 1463 |

## 8. How strong is each claim?

| Strength | Findings |
|---|---|
| **Strong** (text plus independent sources agree) | The same refuge formula redirected (72:6 vs 113:1); 16 of 17 refuges are in Allah; binding vs splitting (ف-ل-ق); night vs daybreak and the dawn/dusk timetable; breath vs breath (*nafth*, Bukhari 5017); the cure credited to Allah, not to undoing the object (Bukhari 5763); no counter-curse (asymmetry); envy as the root motive (Gager's catalogue, al-Qurtubi, 113:5) |
| **Moderate** (fact plus interpretation) | Qalqala closure-release endings; the narrowing order ending on حسد; imperfect أعوذ vs perfect evil verbs; the *falah*/*falaq* pairing (20:64 vs 20:69, 10:77); the psychological layer |
| **Symbolic only** | 11 verses = 11 knots (weak chain); سِحر/سَحَر (separate origins per Ibn Faris); abjad sums (convention-dependent; see Part 1 §7) |
